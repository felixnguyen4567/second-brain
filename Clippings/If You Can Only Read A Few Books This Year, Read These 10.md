## They Aren’t Atomic Habits or The Psychology of Money

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:2000/format:webp/1*KjLHAYb8Y9bPfl1Vuuo8Lg.jpeg)

Source: Photo by Author

I read over 70 books a year. Most of them are fine. Some are forgettable. A handful actually change how you live.

These 10 did that for me. They aren’t the books everyone recommends. You won’t find Atomic Habits here, or The Psychology of Money, or any of the usual suspects that dominate every “best books” list on the internet. Instead, these are the books I kept coming back to, ==the ones that rewired something in my brain and refused to leave.==

If you’re someone who wants to read more but doesn’t have the time, or someone who’s afraid of picking up the wrong book and wasting precious hours, this list is built for you. Every book here earns its spot by being genuinely useful, not just interesting. And for each one, I’m sharing the one thing you should actually do after reading it. Because a book you don’t act on is just entertainment.

*Let’s get into it.*

### #1 Four Thousand Weeks by Oliver Burkeman: You Were Never Meant to Do It All

If you live to 80, you get roughly 4,000 weeks on this planet. Oliver Burkeman opens with that number, and it sits in your chest like a stone. This is not a productivity book. It’s the opposite. Burkeman spent 14 years writing The Guardian’s psychology column, trying every productivity hack in existence, and his conclusion is simple.

Getting more efficient just makes you more rushed. Clearing the decks only makes them fill up faster.

==The idea from this book that changed my life is what Burkeman calls== ==*“cosmic insignificance therapy.”*== ==The realization that you are cosmically irrelevant is not depressing. It’s the most freeing thought you’ll ever have. You don’t have to optimize every hour. You don’t have to do everything. You were never going to.==

This book is the reason I stopped trying to run five projects at once and started focusing on one thing at a time.

> **What to do after reading:** Pick up a pen and write down the things you’re going to deliberately fail at this year. Burkeman calls it strategic underachievement, and it might be the most productive decision you ever make.

### #2 5 Types of Wealth by Sahil Bloom: The Scorecard Nobody Gave You

Sahil Bloom was a Wall Street investor who realized he was winning at the wrong game. His framework splits wealth into five categories. Time wealth comes first, which is the freedom to choose how you spend your hours. Then social wealth, mental wealth, physical wealth, and financial wealth, deliberately placed last.

The concept that hit me hardest is the “time billionaire” idea. If you’re in your 20s, you have roughly 2 billion seconds left. You’re richer than Warren Buffett in the one currency that actually matters. Bloom asks a devastating question in the book.

*Would you trade your entire life for Buffett’s?*

He’s worth $130 billion. But he’s also 95. Nobody takes that trade. And yet we sacrifice time for money every single day without thinking about it.

What makes this book practical instead of just philosophical is the exercises. The Energy Calendar asks you to color-code your weekly activities green, yellow, or red based on whether they energize or drain you, then restructure your life toward green. Takes five minutes.

> ==**What to do after reading:**== ==Do the Energy Calendar exercise this week. Color-code every activity. Then look at how much red is in your life and start cutting.==

### #3 Goals! by Brian Tracy: The Book That Taught Me to Write My Future Down

This book is old school. No fancy frameworks, no viral Twitter threads, no aesthetically pleasing diagrams. Brian Tracy came from nothing. He worked as a laborer, a dishwasher, and a farm hand. He had no formal education. He taught himself success principles by reading three hours a day and turned that into a career advising millions.

His core idea is almost too simple.

*Write your goals down every single morning. Rewrite them fresh without looking at yesterday’s list.*

Tracy says only 3% of adults have written goals, and those who do earn 10 times more than everyone else. I’ve been doing his daily goal-writing practice, and it works in a way that feels almost unfair. It’s the reason I was able to hit specific revenue targets, audience milestones, and eventually quit my job to go full-time as a creator.

You write the goal in the present tense as if it’s already true. “I earn X by December 31.” Then you identify the one goal out of ten that would change everything and work backward from there.

> ==**What to do after reading:**== ==Tonight, write down 10 goals you want to achieve in the next 12 months. Do it in present tense. Circle the one that would make the biggest difference. Rewrite all 10 tomorrow morning.==

### #4 Tiny Experiments by Anne-Laure Le Cunff: Stop Planning Your Life and Start Testing It

Anne-Laure Le Cunff was a Google executive at 27 when doctors found a blood clot in her arm that could have traveled to her lungs. Her first instinct was to keep working. That’s when she knew something was broken. She quit Google, tried a startup, watched it fail, and then said something most people never let themselves say out loud.

*“I admitted that I was lost. And that was the most liberating thought I’d ever had.”*

She went on to do a PhD in Psychology and Neuroscience and wrote this book about replacing rigid life plans with curiosity-driven experiments.

The central tool is the PACT framework. **Purposeful, Actionable, Continuous, and Trackable.** The difference between a PACT and a goal is that a PACT measures output, not outcome. “Did I write for 20 minutes today?” instead of “Did I get 5K subscribers this month?” I used this framework to build consistency in my content business.

Instead of obsessing over numbers, I committed to showing up and running small experiments with topics, formats, and platforms. Some experiments failed. Most taught me something.

> **What to do after reading:** Pick one thing you’ve been overthinking and turn it into a PACT. “I will \[specific action\] for \[specific duration\].” Run it. See what happens.

### #5 Uptime by Laura Mae Martin: What Google’s Productivity Advisor Actually Teaches

Laura Mae Martin has been Google’s Executive Productivity Advisor for over 14 years. She coaches thousands of executives and sends a weekly productivity newsletter to 55,000 Google employees.

The most surprising thing about her definition of productivity is this. She once called a day spent watching TV one of her most productive days, because she had intended to relax and succeeded. ==Productivity, in her world, means your intention matches your action.==

The practical systems are immediately usable. Her List Funnel moves tasks from a master list to a weekly list to a daily list to an hour-by-hour plan, which stops you from drowning in an endless to-do list.

Her Laundry Method for email is brilliant. When you do laundry, you don’t open the dryer and fold one shirt while leaving the rest. But that’s exactly how most people handle their inbox. Instead, sort everything first, then batch-process. Her Meetings Up to PAR framework requires every meeting to have a Purpose, Agenda, and Result, which alone would save most people hours a week.

Martin’s philosophy is refreshingly grounded. She’s not a 20-something tech bro optimizing for hustle. She’s a mother of three who practices No Tech Tuesday with her family.

> **What to do after reading:** Track your energy levels for one week. Find your peak hours. Then protect them like your life depends on it, because your best work does.

### #6 Tools of Titans by Tim Ferriss: 200 Cheat Codes in One Book

Tim Ferriss interviewed over 200 world-class performers on his podcast, which has passed 1 billion downloads, and distilled their best tactics into 707 pages split into three sections. Healthy, Wealthy, and Wise. It’s one of only two books, alongside The Hunger Games, to appear on Amazon’s Top-10 Most Highlighted Books of All Time.

You don’t read this book cover to cover. You use it like a reference manual, flipping to whatever section matches what you’re working on right now.

T ==he most fascinating pattern across all 200 people is that 80% practice daily meditation or mindfulness.== That’s the single most common habit among billionaires, athletes, artists, and scientists. Other patterns include using the same song on repeat for deep focus, skipping breakfast (common among males over 45), and turning obvious weaknesses into competitive advantages.

The criticism is fair. Only 12 out of 114 profiled people are women. Some recommendations feel like cross-promotions for friends’ books. But the sheer density of actionable ideas makes this the best dollar-per-insight book I’ve ever read.

> **What to do after reading:** Don’t read it front to back. Flip to the index, find someone whose life resembles what you’re building, and steal three of their routines this week.

### #7 Good Energy by Dr. Casey Means: Your Body Is Broken, and This Book Explains Why

Dr. Casey Means is a Stanford-trained surgeon who left medicine because she got tired of treating symptoms while ignoring root causes. Her argument is that nearly every chronic disease, from depression and anxiety to heart disease and cancer, shares the same underlying problem. Metabolic dysfunction. The way your cells produce and use energy determines everything.

The most useful part of this book is her breakdown of five biomarkers that actually matter, with optimal ranges that are tighter than what most doctors consider “normal.” Your fasting glucose should be 70 to 85, not the standard “under 99” that labs accept. Your triglycerides should be under 80, not the standard “under 150.”

These numbers alone make the book worth reading because most people walk out of annual checkups thinking they’re fine when their metabolism is quietly falling apart. Her most cited stat is that 93% of American adults have suboptimal metabolic health.

While the action plan at the end is overwhelming, the lifestyle advice itself, though, is solid. Eat real food. Walk after meals. Sleep properly. Get morning sunlight.

> **What to do after reading:** Get blood work done and actually look at the numbers yourself using her optimal ranges instead of the lab’s default “normal” ranges. You might be surprised.

### #8 The Satvic Revolution by Subah & Harshvardhan Saraf: Ancient Wisdom Your Body Has Been Waiting For

This is the book on this list that most people outside India haven’t heard of. Subah and Harshvardhan Saraf are the founders of the Satvic Movement, which has over 5 million YouTube subscribers and 10,000 people joining their health workshops every single month. Both of them healed their own chronic diseases through natural health principles, and this book is the distillation of everything they teach.

The concept of “Satvic” comes from Sanskrit, meaning pure and strong energy. The book presents seven life-changing habits drawn from Ayurveda, the Bhagavad Gita, and modern science.

The first four cover physical health, including plant-based eating, proper sleep aligned with natural rhythms, and movement practices. The last three go where most health books never go, covering mental and spiritual health, resolving unresolved relationships (viewed as emotional toxins), and finding purpose through your innate strengths.

What makes this book stand out is that it costs almost nothing, requires zero technology, and addresses your mind, body, and spirit in a single framework. No CGMs, no expensive supplements, no $500 blood panels. Just ancient principles adjusted for modern life.

> **What to do after reading:** Start with the simplest swap. Replace refined sugar with jaggery, get morning sunlight before checking your phone, and eat your largest meal when the sun is highest.

### #9 Change Your Brain Every Day by Dr. Daniel Amen: 366 Days to a Brain You Don’t Recognize

Dr. Daniel Amen has performed over 300,000 brain scans across 155 countries, which makes his database the largest of its kind in the world. His core message is one line long. You are not stuck with the brain you have. You can make it better, even if you’ve been bad to it.

The book gives you 366 daily entries, one for each day of the year, each with a short lesson and a specific practice. It works like a daily devotional for your brain. His BRIGHT MINDS framework identifies eleven risk factors for brain problems, from blood flow (the #1 predictor of Alzheimer’s) to “diabesity” (as your weight goes up, the size and function of your brain goes down) to sleep (getting fewer than 6 hours switches off approximately 700 protective genes).

The practical advice is genuinely useful regardless of what you think about brain scanning. Take omega-3 fatty acids, because 95% of people don’t get enough. Floss daily, because periodontal disease increases brain inflammation. Before every decision, ask yourself one question: is this good for my brain or bad for it?

The daily format is what makes this different from other brain health books. Each practice builds on the previous ones so that by the end of the year, you’ve created a whole new life.

> **What to do after reading:** Start with the single easiest habit. Before every meal, every purchase, every late night scroll, ask: is this good for my brain or bad for it? That filter alone changes everything.

### #10 The Art and Business of Online Writing by Nicolas Cole: The Book That Changed How I Write Every Single Post

Nicolas Cole started writing online at 17 with a World of Warcraft blog. By his mid-20s, he was the most-read writer on all of Quora out of 200 million users. He went on to accumulate over 100 million views across Medium, Inc, Forbes, Fortune, and Harvard Business Review. This book is him explaining exactly how he did it.

His core belief is that online writing operates under completely different rules than traditional writing. The old model of moving to a cabin and bleeding through a manuscript for years doesn’t work anymore. Two things from this book directly changed my writing.

First, headline engineering. Cole teaches you to create what he calls the “Curiosity Gap,” where your headline tells the reader what the piece is about, who it’s for, and what it promises, all without revealing the answer.

Second, the 1/3/1 writing rhythm, which structures paragraphs using alternating one-sentence and multi-sentence blocks to create a pace that keeps people reading. Every post I write now uses this rhythm.

The criticism is fair. His methods can feel formulaic or clickbaity if you apply them without taste. But Cole addresses that, too. It’s only clickbait if you fail to keep your promise to the reader.

> **What to do after reading:** Write your headline before your next article. If you can’t say what you want to say in one sentence, you won’t be able to say it in a thousand words either.

### The Thread That Ties All 10 Together

These books aren’t random picks. They follow a logic.

Four Thousand Weeks tells you that time is finite, so stop pretending otherwise. Five Types of Wealth helps you decide what’s actually worth pursuing with that time. Goals! gives you a system to go after it. Tiny Experiments keeps you from being rigid about the path you take. Uptime makes your days actually work. Tools of Titans gives you shortcuts from people who’ve already figured it out.

Then the foundation. Good Energy and The Satvic Revolution make sure your body can keep up with your ambitions. Change Your Brain Every Day keeps the organ running the show sharp. And The Art and Business of Online Writing turns everything you’ve learned into something you can share with the world.

Ten books. One for each month, with two months to spare for rereading the ones that hit hardest.

==You don’t need to read 70 books a year. You just need to read the right 10.==
